A shift in a system's internal representations need not change what the system does. OTG measures the operational effect directly. It discovers candidate domains from the system's own representations, matches each input to donors from a reference domain on content descriptors such as labels or masks using entropic optimal transport, replaces the states at chosen interfaces with the donor's, and replays the downstream computation. Each interface or group of interfaces is then labelled harmless, consequential or undetermined against a tolerance band fixed in advance, and OTG abstains when the evidence cannot decide.

Abstract

Modern computer vision systems increasingly comprise interconnected models and processing stages, making it difficult to determine where domain-dependent performance differences arise and whether internal discrepancies have operational consequences. Large representational changes may leave system performance unaffected, while smaller changes or interactions between components can produce consequential effects. We introduce Operational Transport Geometry (OTG), a post-hoc framework for diagnosing domain-dependent behaviour in multi-model systems. OTG discovers candidate domains from internal representations and uses entropic optimal transport to construct counterfactual interventions matched on observed content. By substituting intermediate representations and replaying downstream computation, it estimates the operational effects of individual and joint interface interventions. To avoid exhaustive evaluation of intervention groups, OTG exploits structural blocking relations and decomposes group effects into interaction terms, enabling simultaneous inference under explicit structural assumptions. The framework distinguishes directly evaluated effects from structurally inferred effects, incorporates statistical uncertainty, and abstains when evidence is insufficient. It additionally reports domain-specific treatment comparisons, consequential interface groups, and sensitivity to alternative admissible transport couplings. We evaluate OTG on controlled computational systems and real-world vision pipelines, examining diagnostic correctness, interaction recovery, and computational efficiency.

Three verdicts

Each effect receives a simultaneous confidence interval and is compared with the tolerance band. An interval inside the band gives harmless, an interval wholly outside it gives consequential with a sign, and an interval that straddles an edge gives undetermined. Failing to reject a zero effect is never read as harmlessness.

How OTG works

1. Discover domains

A Gaussian mixture is fitted to low-dimensional summaries at the earliest embedding interface on a separate split of the data. The hard membership map is frozen before any inference.

2. Match on content

Each input is paired with donors from a reference domain through an entropic transport plan over content descriptors, never over internal states, which may carry the very discrepancy under diagnosis.

3. Replay at interfaces

States at a group of interfaces are replaced by those of the donor and the downstream computation is rerun. Blocking relations in the computation graph make many groups redundant, so only a canonical family is replayed, and joint effects are written through interaction terms.

4. Issue verdicts

Simultaneous intervals against the tolerance band give each group a verdict, an evidence label (direct or inferred) and a sensitivity to alternative matchings. Under the stated assumptions, all definitive verdicts are correct simultaneously with probability at least 0.95.

Results

Controlled systems fix the truth by oracle. Recorded systems fix it through a planted fault, an effect with a closed form, or the accuracy gain from fine-tuning a block. Resolution costs are listed beside each result.
